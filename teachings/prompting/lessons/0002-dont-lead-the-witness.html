Prompting · Lesson 2 · Decision-framing

Don't lead the witness

Ask for the decision you're actually making — not the answer you already half-decided.

In Lesson 1 you learned to say what you want and why. This lesson sharpens the "why" into its most useful form and adds the move most people miss entirely. It comes straight out of your own cassette prompt — the one that asked "if there was a CLI/dashboard, would it help…?" Three habits turn that kind of prompt from a rubber stamp into real decision support.

The one win You'll stop getting answers that merely agree with the idea you walked in with, and start getting the recommendation you'd actually act on.

Move 1 — State the decision, not just the question

Lesson 1's "why" was about the goal. Push it one step further: tell the model what you will do with the answer. That's the decision frame, and it changes everything downstream — because the same question has different right answers depending on the choice you're making.

Question only

"Would a dashboard help keep the cassettes up to date?"

Decision framed

"I'm deciding whether to spend this sprint on cassette tooling. A 'win' is cutting the weekly CI flakes from stale cassettes. Given that, is tooling worth it — and if so, the cheapest version that removes the pain?"

Notice the frame does two jobs: it names the choice (build this sprint, or not) and it defines what counts as a win (fewer flakes, not "better" in the abstract). Without those, the model invents its own — usually a generic survey — and you get an answer to a question you weren't asking.

Move 2 — Don't pre-commit to a solution

Your prompt said "if there was a CLI/dashboard." That quietly narrows the model to grading your idea instead of finding the best one. The real question was "how do we keep cassettes fresh?" — and the best answer might be a git hook, a scheduled job, or a staleness lint check, none of which are a dashboard.

Why this matters more with capable models Frontier models like Opus 4.8 and Fable are more likely to follow your framing, not less. They're trained to be helpful and to take instructions literally, so if you hand them a solution to evaluate, they'll evaluate it — thoroughly, and without pushing back that you may be solving the wrong problem. The aperture is yours to set. Set it wide.

The fix is one clause: ask for the space of options first, then offer your candidate as one of them. "Propose the 2–3 lightest ways to keep cassettes fresh, including non-tool options; I've been assuming a dashboard but I want the best fit, not a defense of that."

Move 3 — Ask for a recommendation, not a survey

"What are the pros and cons?" invites both-sidesism: a tidy table where every option looks equally reasonable and you're left to decide anyway. But deciding is the part you wanted help with. A frontier model is a capable thought partner — so make it take a position.

Survey

"What are the pros and cons of each approach?"

Recommendation

"Compare them briefly, then recommend one and commit to it. Give me the single biggest risk of that pick and how I'd know if it's going wrong."

You still get the reasoning — you just also get a decision to react to, which is far easier to sharpen than a neutral list. If you disagree, saying "no, because X" gets you further than picking from a menu.

The three moves, together Frame the decision (what you'll do with the answer + what a win is) · widen the aperture (options first, your idea as one candidate) · demand a position (recommend + name the risk).

Do it once

Here's a fresh leading prompt — different domain on purpose, so it's real practice. Rewrite it with all three moves before you reveal the model answer.

"Should I add Redis caching to the API to make it faster?"
Reveal one strong rewrite (compare the moves, not the wording)

"Our /quote endpoint's p95 is ~1.2s and I need it under 400ms before the launch next month — that's the decision: what gets me there fastest with the least new infra to run? I've been assuming Redis, but I want the best fit, not a defense of it: propose the 2–3 most promising approaches (caching, query changes, precompute, whatever applies), then recommend one and tell me its single biggest risk and how I'd catch it early."

The moves: decision + win-condition (under 400ms before launch) · aperture widened (approaches, Redis demoted to "one assumption") · a position demanded (recommend one + the risk). Notice it also inherits Lesson 1: explicit metric and constraint ("least new infra to run").

When you actually do want a survey Decision-framing is for when you're choosing. If you genuinely want the landscape — you're learning, or briefing someone else — then a neutral pros/cons list is the right ask. The skill is matching the frame to the job, not always demanding a verdict.
