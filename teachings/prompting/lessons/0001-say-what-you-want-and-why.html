Say what you want — and why

The two things beginners leave out, and why they cost you the most re-rolls in Claude Code.

Here's the whole game in one sentence: a model acts on what you actually wrote, not on what you meant. Almost every "Claude did the wrong thing" moment traces back to a requirement that lived in your head and never made it into the prompt. Two habits fix the large majority of these. This lesson is just those two.

The one win By the end you'll be able to look at a request that went sideways and say which of the two levers was missing — instead of just retrying and hoping.

Lever 1 — Explicitness: name the things you'd otherwise assume

A capable model will fill gaps with a reasonable default — which is exactly the problem, because its reasonable default isn't always yours. The fix isn't a longer prompt or a firmer tone. It's naming the specific things you were silently assuming: scope, format, and constraints.

Under-specified

"Clean up the error handling in the auth module."

Explicit

"In auth/session.ts only, replace the bare catch {} blocks with logged, typed errors. Don't touch other files, don't add new dependencies, and keep the existing function signatures."

The second one isn't "more detailed" for its own sake. Every added clause closes a door the model would otherwise have been free to walk through — editing five files, pulling in a library, changing a signature. Anthropic's own guidance calls this being clear, direct, and detailed, and it's the single highest-return beginner technique.

Why this bites harder in Claude Code In a one-off chat, a vague answer costs you a re-read. In an agent loop, a vague instruction costs you actions — files edited, commands run — before you notice. Explicitness is cheap insurance against work you have to undo.

Lever 2 — Intent: tell it why, not just what

You cannot enumerate every case. The moment the model hits a situation your prompt didn't cover, it has to guess — and its guess is only as good as its understanding of what you're trying to achieve. Give it the intent, and it generalizes in the direction you actually want.

What only

"Summarize this meeting note in 5 bullets."

What + why

"Summarize this meeting note in ~5 bullets. It's going into a project wiki page that future-me reads to remember decisions — so favor decisions made and open questions over who-said-what."

With the "why," the model knows what to keep when it must choose. This is also the fix for the classic skill-authoring failure: an instruction like "be concise" gets interpreted a dozen ways, but "be concise because this renders in a narrow terminal and the reader has been watching you work" tells it which kind of concise. State the goal; let the model reason to the specifics.

Rule of thumb Explicitness handles the cases you can foresee. Intent handles the ones you can't. You almost always want both.

Check yourself

1. In Claude Code you asked it to fix one file and it reformatted five. Most likely, the prompt was missing —

2. Why does telling Claude the reason behind a request help most?

Do it once

Here is a real-shaped, weak prompt. Rewrite it so it carries both levers — explicit scope/format/constraints, and the intent behind it. Do it in your head or on paper before you reveal the model answer.

"Write a CLAUDE.md rule so Claude stops leaving TODO comments."
Reveal one strong rewrite (yours may differ — compare the moves, not the words)

"Add a rule to CLAUDE.md: never leave TODO/FIXME comments in code you write — either implement it now or, if it's genuinely out of scope, raise it to me in chat instead. Why: this repo ships to production from main and stray TODOs rot silently; I'd rather decide explicitly than discover them months later. Keep the rule to two sentences so it stays scannable among the other rules."

Notice the moves: explicit trigger (TODO/FIXME), explicit alternative behavior (implement or escalate), the why (so the model generalizes to cases like a half-finished helper), and a format constraint (two sentences) so the rule fits its neighbors.

A caveat you'll unlearn later, on purpose "Explicit" does not mean "shout." Piling on CRITICAL: YOU MUST ALWAYS… actually backfires on current Opus and Fable models — they follow instructions literally and over-trigger on forceful language. Clarity ≠ volume.
